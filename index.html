Video teamWhere synthetic video has got to · 10 September 2026

Too good to be
true.

Eighteen months ago a generated video was easy to spot. The tells we taught the newsroom have largely gone, automated detection now performs worse than guessing, and the provenance systems being announced, Apple's included, are built for photographs. What follows is what changed, what did not, and what it means for how we work.

For discussion

Too good to be true 01 / NN

The argument

Three propositions. This session is worth having if the room disagrees with at least one.

The models arrived

In eighteen months generative video gained sound, plausible physics, faces that survive a cut, and environments that can be navigated. Each advance retired a category of tell.

Detection did not

Every substantial test published this year finds automated detection unreliable at the point of use. This is not a technical problem awaiting a technical fix, and none is in prospect.

The fixes are for photographs

SynthID, Content Credentials and Apple Reference Image point the same way, and all are strongest on stills. Video is the poor relation in every announcement of the past year.

The working assumption for this team: nothing on the horizon allows us to verify a video by looking at it, or by uploading it somewhere. Established verification - who supplied it, where they were, what corroborates it, and whether it happens to say precisely what somebody wanted said - is the method rather than the fallback.

Too good to be true · Part 1 06 / NN

Part 1 · What it cost us

The tells we lost.

The same four releases, read from our side of the desk. Each retired a check that had been good enough to settle an argument.

Silence

Until May 2025 generated video arrived mute, and convincing synchronised sound meant a microphone had been involved. One release removed that. Lip-sync across a multi-shot sequence, in five languages, is now a product feature.

Physics

Water that misbehaves, cloth that floats, a ball that fails to rebound. The strongest of the surviving tells, which says rather more about the others than it does about this one.

The same face twice

Holding a recognisable person across a cut was close to impossible, and a changed collar or a watch on the wrong wrist was a gift. Consistent characters across multi-shot sequences are now routine.

One fixed camera

Early models managed a locked-off shot or a slow drift, and a specific camera move broke them. Camera control is now a parameter, and Atlas will hold a specified move for a minute.

Too good to be true · Part 1 07 / NN

Part 1 · The bit that isn't a clip

When video becomes a place.

The shift most likely to matter to us, and the least discussed. A world model does not generate a video. It generates an environment, and the video is what a camera moving through it produces.

  1. 01

    You can walk around inside it

    Genie 3, August 2025. Each frame is generated from the prompt and from what the viewer has just done, so the place responds in real time. 720p, 24fps, coherent for minutes.

    Sold as Project Genie in Google's AI Ultra tier at $200 a month. A consumer product, not a research demo.

  2. 02

    You can export it and take it away

    Marble, November 2025. Text, a photograph or a panorama becomes a 3D environment that can be edited and downloaded, with a public API since January.

    The output is not a clip someone might notice is wrong. It is an asset, to be lit, shot and cut like any other.

  3. 03

    And now it is general

    Atlas, nine days ago. Trained from scratch to work natively across text, images, video and 3D at once, producing a minute of camera-controlled 1440p.

    The gap between a video model and a world model is closing, and the world model is the one that wins.

Why this matters for verification. A generated clip fell apart under a camera move because it was invented frame by frame with no scene underneath. A world model has one, so it is internally consistent by construction, and a whole family of our tells goes with it.

Too good to be true · Part 2 10 / NN

Part 2 · Where to look, and where not to

Live tells, dead tells.

The right-hand column matters more. Advice that was sound two years ago is still circulating, and acting on it now produces confidence rather than accuracy.

Still worth checking

None of these is proof. They mark where the models remain measurably weaker.

  • LengthQuality degrades past twenty to twenty-five seconds. Convincing fakes are short, or cut.
  • Bodies in contact, and crowdsHandshakes, embraces, several interacting subjects. Coherence fails fastest wherever people meet.
  • Text in shotSigns, badges, plates, documents, screens. Often garbled, and unusually easy to check.
  • Water and clothSpray, flags, loose clothing. The physics is plausible rather than correct.
  • Continuity across a cutA watch that changes wrist, jewellery that arrives and leaves.

No longer worth your time

All were sound advice within the last two years. None will reliably catch a current model.

  • Count the fingersThe standard demo failure, now routinely correct. It has also backfired, as the next slide shows.
  • Asymmetric eyes, misaligned ears, painted-on teethThe 2023 signature. The same prompts now produce faces nobody queries.
  • Mute it and see if it holds upNative audio since May 2025.
  • Check the resolution4K is standard. Genuine phone footage of a real event is usually worse.
  • Run it through a detectorTwo slides on, with the numbers.

Part 2 · Automated detection

Nobody can do this.

NewsGuard built twenty AI videos of documented false claims, removed the watermarks with an off-the-shelf tool, and asked three chatbots twice per video whether what they were looking at was real. These are the rates at which they answered wrongly.

95%

Grok

Wrong on 38 of 40 prompts. Still wrong 30% of the time with the watermark left in place.

92.5%

ChatGPT

37 of 40. OpenAI's own position: it “does not have the ability to determine whether content is AI-generated”.

78%

Gemini

The best of the three. With the watermark present it answered every question correctly, which locates the capability in the watermark.

75.5%

Us

People shown high-quality deepfake video under test conditions identified it correctly 24.5% of the time.

A note on the figures. The three machine results are a single study, forty prompts per model, and they measure a general-purpose chatbot being asked a question rather than a purpose-built forensic tool. The human figure comes from separate research on a different task and should be read as an order of magnitude rather than a like-for-like comparison. What survives the caveats is the shape: with the watermark gone, every result here is at or below guesswork, and the machines are no better than we are.

Too good to be true · Part 3 14 / NN

Part 3 · Three different jobs

Three systems, three claims.

These are talked about in one breath and answer entirely different questions. Note which of them is about video.

01

SynthID

An invisible watermark placed in the pixels, and in audio and text, by the model that generated them. Google's, now adopted by OpenAI, Nvidia, ElevenLabs and Kakao, with over 100 billion assets marked. Veo marks every frame. Its claim: a participating model made this.

02

Content Credentials

Cryptographically signed metadata travelling with the file: what created it, what edited it, and when. Signed at capture by Leica, Sony, Nikon, Canon, Samsung and Pixel, and TikTok joined the steering committee in July. Its claim: here is the file's history, if nobody removed it.

03

Apple Reference Image

Announced last night. A camera sensor that signs what it sees at pixel level, developed by Private Cloud Compute into an unalterable reference image held in Photos beside the shot. Its claim: these pixels came off a physical sensor and are unaltered.

The claim none of them makes

Not one of the three says “this is true”. Two are statements about a tool and one is a statement about a sensor. A signed, watermark-free, sensor-verified video of an event that never happened remains entirely possible: point the signing camera at a good monitor.

Too good to be true · Part 3 15 / NN

Part 3 · Last night, Cupertino

A sensor that signs every pixel.

Apple's own wording. In Reference mode the signed sensor data becomes an unalterable reference image, a digital negative to hold against whatever was published. The most substantial thing shipped in this area, and explicitly aimed at photojournalists. Four qualifications.

  1. 01

    It is photographs. Not video.

    The announcement concerns images throughout; there is no video component in it. For this team the headline feature of the year's largest provenance launch is, today, not a feature. Whether it reaches video, and when, is the most useful question anyone here could ask.

  2. 02

    It needs new hardware, and it is opt-in

    A new sensor, so it will not arrive by software update on any existing handset, and the photographer must be in Reference mode at the moment of capture. The picture that matters is usually taken by someone not expecting to take it, on whatever they had.

  3. 03

    Not everywhere, and not immediately

    Ships with iPhone 18 Pro from 18 September. No capture at launch in China or the EU, where iOS 27 can view reference images but not produce them. Outside our jurisdiction, but not outside our European colleagues'.

  4. 04

    It proves capture, not truth

    These pixels came off this sensor unaltered. It cannot establish that the sensor was pointed at something real, and says nothing about caption or claim, where most of our errors originate. Nor about the oldest trick there is: filming a screen.

Too good to be true · Part 3 16 / NN

Part 3 · The hole in all of it

All of this is optional.

Each system is defeated by something readily available, and the defeats differ in kind. One rule follows from all four: a mark present is evidence; a mark absent is nothing.

Metadata is removable

Content Credentials are metadata. A screenshot, a re-encode, or any platform that strips EXIF removes the history, and there are applications built to do precisely that. The absence of a credential carries no information at all.

Watermarks degrade

SynthID is distributed across the whole frame and survives a 30% crop and heavy compression, which is good engineering. It does not survive everything. The watermarks in the study two slides back came out with a consumer web tool.

Only participants participate

SynthID marks the output of participating models. An open-weights model running on private hardware marks nothing, costs nothing and is good enough. These systems cover the well-behaved end of the market, which is not where the problem is.

And then the analogue hole

Play the fake on a good monitor. Film the monitor with a signing camera. The result is cryptographically signed, watermark-free provenance for a falsehood. That is not a flaw in anyone's implementation; it is the boundary of what a signature can mean.

Too good to be true · Part 3 17 / NN

Part 3 · Since 2 August

Disclosure is now a legal duty.

The EU AI Act's transparency article took effect five weeks ago. It does not bind us, and it explains the timing of everything else in this section.

What it says

Article 50 applies from 2 August 2026. Anyone deploying an AI system to create a deepfake must disclose that the content has been artificially generated or manipulated, and the obligation does not depend on any intent to deceive. The Commission published guidelines on 20 July, and penalties reach €15m or 3% of worldwide turnover.

What it changes for us

Little, directly. The exemption in the drafting covers evidently artistic, creative, satirical or fictional work, which is not a journalism exemption; if it ever bears on a piece it needs proper advice rather than a slide. What the Act does is make machine-readable labelling commercially unavoidable for the large providers, which is much of why SynthID adoption accelerated this year.

The uncomfortable reading. The marks now appearing in the pixels are a response to regulation and liability rather than a decision that provenance was worth having. That works either way, but it indicates where the marks will stop: at the edge of what is enforceable, which is well short of where our problem sits.

Too good to be true · Part 4 19 / NN

Part 4 · The method that still works · 1 of 2

Start with the people.

In order of value, and the order is the argument. These two come first because they rest on judgement, which is the part that has not been automated. The tools appear on the next slide, last.

  1. 01

    Provenance of the person, not the file

    Who supplied this, how did they come by it, what else have they sent us, and will they take a call. A model can imitate footage. It cannot imitate a person with a history who answers the phone. This is the only check on the list that has increased in value, and it costs time that is rarely budgeted for.

    “Who filmed this, on what, and can you send me the original from the phone rather than the copy from WhatsApp?”

  2. 02

    The too-good-to-be-true test

    Does the material land precisely on what somebody wants to be true, ourselves included? Fabrications are built to be shared, so they tend to be unusually well composed and unusually convenient. After Maduro's removal in January, the AI footage in circulation showed crowds celebrating exactly what those sharing it wanted celebrated. Fox News presented one as genuine before withdrawing the article.

    “If this is real, who benefits from my believing it, and does that make me want it to be real?”

Too good to be true · Part 4 20 / NN

Part 4 · The method that still works · 2 of 2

Then, and only then, the machines.

  1. 03

    Corroborate outside the frame

    Light direction and shadow length against the stated time. Signage, plates, uniforms, weather and architecture against the stated place. Other footage of the same moment from another angle. A generated clip has no other angle, and no second source that agrees with it in detail. This is where the surviving tells belong: a reason to look harder, never a verdict.

    “What else must be true if this is true, and can any of it be checked independently?”

  2. 04

    And then, if it helps, the tools

    Look for Content Credentials. Run a SynthID check. Use a detector. A positive result is a genuinely useful lead. A negative result carries almost no information, and the figures in Part 2 are why. The rule that follows: a clean detector score must never be the reason we published.

    “Would I still run this if the detector had returned the opposite answer?”

The operational gap. All of the above assumes a handful of items a week need this treatment. The week when many of them do requires two decisions taken in advance: that “we have been unable to verify this footage” is publishable copy, and that a named person on shift may say it on deadline without escalating to three people who are asleep.

Too good to be true · Part 4 21 / NN

Part 4 · The risk that runs the other way

The bigger risk isn't the fake.

Discussion of this defaults to the fabrication we might publish. The failure occurring more often, and the one we are least organised for, is genuine material being dismissed. Legal scholars named it the liar's dividend in 2018, before any of this worked.

A vice-presidential candidate in Ghana

A genuine video, with clear authenticity markers, dismissed as a deepfake. The doubt did the work the fake would have done, at no cost to whoever raised it.

Footage from a White House window

The material went viral, was confirmed genuine by the President's own team, and was then attributed to AI by the President. The confirmation was already on the record and made no difference.

The proof-of-life video

Netanyahu in March, obliged to display a five-fingered hand to answer people who believed his own footage was synthetic. When the denial is available to everyone, everyone uses it, including those telling the truth.

What it costs

Renee Hobbs's formulation: the danger is not only that people are deceived, but that they stop caring whether something is true, which is worse. Our masthead is a claim about precisely that, and it is worth more in this environment, not less.

Part 4 · Over to you

Six questions for the room.

None has a settled answer. A starting position is attached to each, to argue with.

  1. 01
    Do we label our own use, and where?The BBC's position: audiences should never feel misled about where AI has been applied. Ours: at the point of viewing, not in a policy, and never a blanket notice
  2. 02
    What is our threshold for video we cannot verify?That “we have been unable to verify this footage” becomes ordinary publishable copy rather than grounds to spike, or we lose to those with no threshold at all
  3. 03
    Do we buy any of this tooling?Nothing on detectors. Something on the unglamorous end: a fast route to originals from a source's phone, and somewhere to keep the working
  4. 04
    Who decides at eleven at night?The decisions that will damage us will not be taken in a meeting. Do the people on late shifts know what they may say on air, and whom they wake?
  5. 05
    Do we generate anything, ever?Reconstruction, illustration, anonymising a contributor. The boundary is not obvious, and better drawn deliberately than discovered
  6. 06
    What happens the first time we are the target?Not if. A convincing fake of a correspondent, or of our own output carrying our branding. That response must exist before the day it is needed

Appendix · A draft for discussion

Five lines, to be amended.

A first attempt at something short enough to put on a wall. Offered as a starting point, not a proposal.

  • 01

    The source outranks the file

    No clip we cannot trace to a person is published without the words that say so.

  • 02

    A detector is a lead, never a clearance

    We may act on a positive result. We never rely on a negative one, and we never cite it as our reason.

  • 03

    Always ask for the original

    From the device, not the platform. As reflex, on everything, before it is the item that matters.

  • 04

    State what is not known

    “We have been unable to verify this footage” is publishable. Silence and borrowed confidence are not.

  • 05

    Authenticating our own work is half the job

    When our footage is called a fake, we need to show the working within the hour. That is a filing discipline, not a technology.
